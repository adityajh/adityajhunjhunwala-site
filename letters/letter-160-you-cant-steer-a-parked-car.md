# Letter 160. You can't steer a parked car.

Dear friend,

I love a long drive. There is something about two people in the front seat of a car, facing the same way, watching the road come at them, and talking. Aarti told me something she noticed about our teenagers. You know how teenagers answer. Hmm. Haan. Good. Fine. Whole sentences rationed out like they cost money. But in the car, she said, they open up. They tell her about their friends, their day, the things they are actually thinking. So she started driving them around herself instead of sending them with the driver. I have started doing the same.

I think it is the geometry of it. You are not looking at each other. You are looking at a moving scene. The engine hums up through the seat and into your body. Something about that combination loosens the tongue. If there is someone you have been wanting a deeper conversation with, try it. Put them in the passenger seat and just drive.

But that is not actually what I sat down to write to you about. I got distracted. Let me get to it.


------


Imagine you are on one of those long drives, and somewhere along the way you lose your road. So you stop. You look around. You check the map. You even make a call or two. And it becomes clear. You are pointed the wrong way. You have to turn around.

So what do you do?

Obvious, you say. You turn the car around. Fine. But here is my actual question. Will you rotate the car a full 180 on the spot? Picture it. The car is standing still in the middle of the road. Can it turn? No car on earth can change direction while it is stationary. You have to put it in gear, roll forward a little, find some room, and only then can you begin the turn.

It seems too obvious to say out loud.

And yet in our lives, we forget it completely.


------


When we feel lost in life, we do exactly the right first things. We halt. We think. We check our maps. We talk to people who have driven this road before. And often we arrive at the honest realisation that we need to change direction.

Sometimes it is a small correction. A musician who realises she wants to play live far more than she wants to sit in a studio. Sometimes it is a sharper turn. An engineering student who discovers he does not much like telecom, and that his real pull is towards civil. A real change, but a change of lane, not of road. And sometimes it is close to a full 180. The same student realising he does not want engineering at all, he wants to make films.

Fine tune, lane change, or 180. My question is the same for all three.

Can you turn a parked car?


------


In this little story, the car is you. The road is the whole world of what is possible. The long drive is your life. And the car, this specific car, is today. It is this moment. It is what you actually do right now.

Here is the whole point. No matter which way you need to turn, you cannot move a stationary vehicle. You have to put yourself in gear and roll forward a little first. Only once you are moving, only once you find some open space, can you actually begin to steer. You might have to change lanes. You might have to hug the left and wait for a gap. You might have to drive on a few more kilometres and take a different route home. In every single case, the car has to be moving.

A parked car cannot change direction.

We have to keep moving.


------


And here is the other thing I have come to believe. Life is a long drive. It is not a trip from A to B. It is not a run to the grocery store or the cinema or a friend's house, where the destination is fixed and known and the only real question is how to get there. In a real life the destination is not fixed. It moves. It grows as you grow. The place you were so sure you were driving towards at twenty-two is rarely the place waiting for you at forty.

Which is why I want to say this to you plainly.

Finding *a* direction matters far more than finding *the* direction.

You will not find the one true road by sitting parked at the side of it, engine off, waiting for certainty to knock on your window. You find it by driving. By choosing a direction that is good enough for now, rolling forward, and letting the road correct you. And you can only correct while you are moving.

A novelist, E.L. Doctorow, once described writing a book like this. It is like driving at night in the fog, he said. You can only see as far as your headlights, but you can make the whole trip that way. I have never read a better description of a life. You are not handed the whole road. You are handed the next small stretch of it, lit just enough to keep going. That was never a reason to stop the car. It is the only way anyone has ever arrived anywhere.


------


And this is not just a comforting thing to say. It is close to how we are actually built, and the research here is worth carrying in your pocket.

When someone is stuck in the dark, in a real, heavy depression, one of the treatments that works best does something that sounds almost too simple to matter. It is called behavioural activation, and it does not wait for the person to feel like living again. It gets them gently doing first. A walk. A meal cooked. One small action, before any motivation is anywhere in sight. In a landmark trial it held its own against antidepressant medication. The feeling, it turns out, follows the doing, and not the other way around.

There is an even harder number. In 2019, researchers writing in JAMA Psychiatry used people's own genetics to untangle cause from effect, which is about as close to proof as a question like this allows. They found that a real step up in daily physical movement was linked to roughly a twenty-six percent lower risk of depression. And crucially, the arrow ran from moving to feeling better, not the reverse. Sitting still and waiting to feel like it is, quite literally, the harder road. Sometimes the shortest way out of your own head is through your legs.

So when I say keep the car moving, I do not only mean it as a metaphor. We keep believing we have to feel ready before we move. Mostly, we move, and the readiness quietly catches up.


------


I was reminded of all this last week, from the other side of the table.

I have been mentoring someone who has been circling the same painful question for months. Which path. Which one. The right one. Near the end of one of our conversations, without any prompting from me, she wrote a line down for herself. Not for me. For herself. She wrote, "It is better to choose a direction than to choose the direction." And under it, "Choosing one path made me feel lighter."

That was almost word for word the line I had been scribbling in my own notebook that same week. I did not teach it to her. She arrived at it on her own, out of her own life, which made me trust it more, not less. The idea is in the air right now. Choose *a* direction. Not *the* direction.


------


You can watch the very same thing in a life many of you already know a little.

Before Kunal Shah was a name in the headlines, he was a philosophy student at a Mumbai college, which is nobody's idea of a straight road to anything. His family hit money trouble when he was young, so from his teens he was already trying things, small hustles, a dozen little ventures, none of them the plan. When he finished college he did not join the family business. He took a junior job at a small outsourcing firm and spent years there learning how things actually get built. He even enrolled in an MBA and then walked out of it, saying he was better off learning on his own than sitting in a programme he felt was built for scoring marks and not for really understanding things. His first proper venture, a cashback company called PaisaBack, did not work. So he turned the wheel, kept the car moving, and that failed idea became FreeCharge.

None of those was the direction. Each was just a direction, good enough to move on, until the next one showed itself. And the moving never stopped. This June, decades after that philosophy degree, Meta sent him a cold email out of nowhere and asked him to lead WhatsApp, used by more people in this country than anywhere on earth. He could not have planned that at twenty. The direction came looking, and it found a man who had never once parked the car.


------


And I want to say one thing to the youngest people reading this, because the timing is not lost on me.

Right now, as I write, a few million teenagers in this country are living inside exactly this fear. After the exam-leak headlines this year, the biggest medical entrance was cancelled and held all over again, and lakhs of students are still waiting, tonight, to even learn what became of them. Others have just watched a single result land and felt a door they trained years for go quietly shut. If that is you, or your child, I want to say this as gently as I know how. That result is not the road. It is one stretch of it, lit for a moment in the fog. It feels like the whole map because right now it is all the light you have been given. It is not. You are allowed to choose a direction that is only good enough for this one year, put the car in gear, and trust that the road will keep offering you turns you cannot yet see. The exam was never the destination. It was one lane on a very long drive. Keep moving.


------


So here is the one thing to actually do this week, not just nod along to.

Find the place in your life where you have been parked. The decision you keep circling. The move you keep researching instead of making. And take one small, real step in a direction, precisely because you are not fully sure it is the right one. Not the whole 180. Just enough forward motion to give yourself something to steer. Change one lane. Make one call. Ship one ugly first version. Put the car in gear.

Because you were never going to think your way to the perfect road. You were going to drive your way to it. Slow down, yes. Experiment, question, discuss, of course. But do not let the fear grow so large that you switch the engine off. The lost do not need more maps. They need to keep moving.

You will find your direction.

Or, if I am honest with you, your direction will find you.

But only if you are moving when it comes looking.

I made baked beans this week. But not straight from the tin.

A tin of baked beans is somebody else's finished decision. You could just heat it and eat it, and plenty of people do. That is the food version of driving somebody else's route and calling it your own life. So I do not stop at the tin. I melt a generous knob of Amul butter in a pan and soften a good handful of chopped onions in it, slowly, keeping them moving so they turn sweet and gold instead of catching. Then the beans go in, and the butter and the onions and the sauce come together into something that is still baked beans, and is also, unmistakably, mine.

The tin got me moving. The butter and the onions were me putting the car in gear.

That is the drive, the wrong turn, the parked car and the beans, all saying one quiet thing. You do not have to invent the whole road from scratch. You are allowed to start with a direction someone else already paved. Just do not sit in the tin. Add your own turn, keep it moving, and let the moving make it yours.

So, one question, and please just hit reply and answer it in a single line. What is the one small step you are going to take this week to get the car rolling again? That is the whole reply. I read every single one.
