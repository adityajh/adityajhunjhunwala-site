# Letter 159. Someone is going to lead you.

Dear friend,

A few weeks ago I was working out of the new IKEA that has opened at Phoenix Mall here in Pune. The coffee is ninety rupees and they let you refill it endlessly, which for someone like me is less an offer than a dare, so I had set up at a table for a few hours. At some point I finished my nth cup, stood up, and walked off, leaving it on the table. The way I always have. The way, if I am honest, I'm used to. Someone else clears the cup.

A lady working there caught my eye. She did not pick it up. She gestured, with a gentle smile, for me to carry it to the bin myself.

She had probably spent her whole working life clearing up after people like me. Every instinct she owned said take the cup. But she didn't. She had a standard of her own, and she dared to hold it on me, a grown man with twice her status in that room and the table manners of a child. I felt about two feet tall. I carried my cup. And in those ten quiet seconds, a woman with no title had shown me something, and quietly edited a default I had been dragging around for forty years.


------


Now hold her next to last week.

I bought a packet of beans from Veronica's in Mumbai. They have the best burgers. There's usually a wait of thirty to forty-five minutes. There's something special about the place. Recently they opened a new restaurant in Lower Parel; the original is in Bandra, and that's where we went. We loved our burgers. We enjoyed chatting with the waiter. As we were leaving, I remembered they also sell coffee beans. There was a manager standing near the counter, and I asked him if they were fresh. "Roasted just this morning," he said. I believed him completely. I paid, and walked home already deciding which mug.

Then I turned the packet over. Roasted 1st June. That day was the 27th.

I know exactly how this sounds. It does not even matter. Coffee stays good for about six weeks, the beans were perfectly fine. But something in me went cold anyway. It was a small, silly thing to mind, and I minded it. I had been lied to over nothing, when the truth would have cost him absolutely nothing. 

The way I looked at Veronica's hasn't changed yet. But I was surprised.

The difference between the lady at IKEA and the manager at Veronica's was not rank. She had a way of her own, and she dared to stand on it. He did not, so he reached for whatever was easiest and let that lead him. And that, I have come to think, is the whole question of a life. Do you have a way of your own? Or will you be led, quietly, down someone else's?


------


For years I would have given you a very confident answer. Follow no one. Find your own way. I was rather proud of that line. It is also not quite true. Almost nobody finds their own way sitting alone in an empty room. We need someone to go first, to show us the thing can even be done. The final choice is always, always ours. But we usually arrive at it by watching someone who already dared to make theirs.

So the question was never whether to follow. It is who.


------


And this is why I have stopped believing we need more politicians. We need more artists and scientists and musicians and people who actually build things. We need more ladies at the IKEA counter.

Think about what those people actually do. An artist dares to know herself, and then dares to show herself in public. A scientist stakes a claim on what is true and then stands in front of it. They never hand you an instruction. They do something braver. They go first, as themselves, where everyone can see. And when you watch someone do that, something odd happens. You do not come away wanting to copy them. You come away wanting to go and find your own thing.

The people who actually changed my life never once told me what to do. They just let me watch them be unmistakably themselves, and it sent me looking inward. That is what a real leader does. They do not point the way. They stand in it, until a roomful of people quietly go looking for their own.


------


The politician runs on the opposite fuel.

He does not need you to know your own way. He needs you not to. Someone who knows where they are going is almost impossible to lead anywhere else. Someone who does not will follow whoever is loudest, and feel grateful for the direction.

You will remember Alice at the fork, asking the Cheshire Cat which way to go. "That depends a good deal on where you want to get to," he says. "I don't much care where," says Alice. "Then it doesn't much matter which way you go." And then the line that should unsettle all of us. "Oh, you're sure to get somewhere, if you only walk long enough."

You will get somewhere. That is the trap. The lost do not stop walking. They simply get walked to someone else's somewhere. The directionless are never left alone for long. They get led. And then they get used.


------


I have been watching exactly this play out, live, in our own country.

You have probably seen them. When the system called its unemployed young people cockroaches, they did not flinch. They took the word, put on the costume, went out and cleaned the rivers, and wrote down in plain language exactly what was broken. I have spent ten years saying our young people are not lazy, they are hungry, and there they were, proving it, in costume, with a broom in hand. The Cockroach Janta Party began the way the best things do. As artists and rebels and whistleblowers. They dared to show themselves, and millions of young people saw them and finally felt seen. They showed a way.

A month in, they are standing at the very fork this letter is about. So far they have refused to register as a party, refused to play the old media games. But they have also begun issuing ultimatums and demanding resignations, the old grammar of power. I am rooting for them with my whole heart, and I am holding my breath. Because the real danger for anyone who dares to show the way is not failure. It is slowly, and without noticing, becoming the very thing they rose against.


------


I do not know yet which way they will turn. But I know it can be done, because this city has shown me once already.

Dr Grant built one of the finest hospitals in the country right here in Pune, starting from four beds in a rented building. He became one of India's earliest and best cardiologists. He once survived a plane hijacking, took a bullet in the knee, and lived and worked another twenty-five years. And the story people here still tell is that even near eighty, the founder of a five-hundred-bed hospital would still walk from his home to its wards each morning, on his own two feet. He had built something enormous, and it had not made him grand. He never needed a title to remember who he was.

That is a man who held his own way to the very end.


------


You and I are at the same fork. Smaller, quieter, and every single day.

Someone is going to shape which way you turn. That is not weakness, it is being human. The work is to make sure the people you let in are the ones daring to show you themselves, not the ones who need you lost. And to build a way of your own, the way the lady at the counter had hers, so that when the quiet, unwatched second comes, you already know which way you turn.


------


So here is the one thing to do this week, not just nod at.

Notice who you are actually following. Not who you say you admire. Who is shaping which way you turn, the voices in your feed, the people whose approval you quietly chase. Ask one honest question of each. Is this person daring to show me who they are, or just telling me where to go? Keep the first. Mute the second. Then go find one small way of your own, and stand on it out loud, where someone can see.

I made a plain yellow dal this week.

Toor dal, turmeric, salt, water, soft after the better part of an hour. Beige, sensible, forgettable on its own.

Then the tadka, which is honestly the only part of cooking I look forward to. Ghee till it shimmers, cumin till it crackles, slivers of garlic till they just turn gold, a pinch of hing, a dried red chilli, a spoon of red chilli pulled off the heat so it blooms red and not black. Poured over the pot so the whole thing hisses and goes up in smell. Fifteen seconds, and the beige pot becomes the dish.

A recipe is a way someone paved before you, so you do not have to start from nothing. Follow it. But the tadka is the part no recipe can take from you. You stand at the stove with the garlic turning from gold to burnt in the space of three seconds, and nobody on earth can choose that moment but you. You were shown the way. The final turn is still yours.

That is the lady, the coffee, the cockroaches, the old doctor, and the dal, all asking one question. Follow the ones brave enough to show you themselves. And then dare to find, and to stand on, a way of your own.

Write back and tell me whose way you have been walking, and what your own would look like. I read every reply.

In fratitude (friendship and gratitude),

Adi
